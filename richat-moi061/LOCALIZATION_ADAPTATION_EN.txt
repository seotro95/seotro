## 1
A layer of desert rock rises at an angle and disappears along a curved ridge. Why does its edge belong to a circle about forty kilometers wide? Another ridge stands beyond it, then another. Seen from above, they form the Eye of the Sahara. Our central question is what raised these layers, exposed their edges as rings, and left the pattern visible in the desert. The first discovery is concrete: the rings follow natural layers in the ground. Different rocks resist erosion differently. The formation records uplift and the removal of material. Richat lies in Mauritania, on the Adrar Plateau. Its enormous size makes the complete pattern difficult to recognize from ground level. A satellite image reconnects ridges that can look separate when we walk among them. The center exposes older sedimentary layers, surrounded by younger layers. This arrangement points toward an eroded dome. Picture a stack bent upward, then cut across its upper part. The curved layers appear as nested bands on the exposed surface. Missing material matters as much as the remaining stone. French geographers described the formation in the nineteen thirties. Spaceflight later made its immense outline internationally familiar. The important change was viewpoint. The rocks already carried the story. Imagine following a layer from the ridge into the ground: its tilt now becomes a physical clue to the structure. We have an outline and a geological problem to test. A photograph shows the pattern, while field evidence must establish its origin; circular shape alone does not settle the question. How can lifting a stack of flat layers produce rings without drawing a circle on the ground?

## 4
A dark sheet lies between pale sedimentary beds. Magma entered the crust, spread through available space, and cooled into a solid body. A sheet following the bedding is a sill. Richat contains two prominent gabbroic bodies with curved surface outlines. Gabbro is an igneous rock with coarse crystals formed as magma cooled underground. Earlier interpretations treated these bodies as ring dykes related to a central intrusion. Later work explored whether they were older sills lifted with their surrounding sedimentary rocks. That alternative changes the sequence of events. A study published in the year two thousand twenty-four added chemical analyses and argon-based work on the ages of the gabbros. The authors proposed that the Richat bodies first entered the crust as sills. A younger intrusion could then raise those older sheets and the surrounding layers into a dome. The familiar outline would therefore contain rock emplaced well before the main uplift. One surface pattern becomes a longer sequence. ## 5
